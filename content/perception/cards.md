## Attention

> You can't notice everything, so attention is selective. That's why you can miss a change (change blindness) or an object right in front of you (inattentional blindness), yet still hear your name across a noisy room (cocktail party effect) or spot the one odd item (pop-out).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Selective attention | Focusing your conscious awareness on a particular stimulus and NOT something else. |  |
| Stroop effect | Delayed reaction time when you must say the color of a word but not the name of the word. Example from your slides: the word GREEN printed in purple, where you have to say "purple." | The word fights the color. |
| Change blindness | Failing to notice changes in the environment because your attention is directed elsewhere. | Missed a CHANGE. |
| Inattentional blindness | Failing to see visible objects when your attention is directed elsewhere. | Missed a THING that was right there. |
| Change vs. inattentional blindness | Both happen because attention is directed elsewhere. Change blindness = not noticing that something changed. Inattentional blindness = not seeing a visible object at all. |  |
| Cocktail party effect | The ability to focus auditory attention on a particular voice or sound while filtering out others. In a crowded room, you can focus on the voice of the one person you're talking with. |  |
| Hearing your name | Part of the cocktail party effect: when we hear our name, our brain can register it even amongst background noise. |  |
| Pop-out phenomenon | Something different popping out to get our attention, like the one laughing face in the cartoon of a crying movie audience on your slides. |  |

## Top-down vs. bottom-up

> Bottom-up processing starts with the senses sending information up to the brain (sensation). Top-down processing uses knowledge, experience and context to interpret it (perception), as when you read messy handwriting or scrambled words.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Bottom-up processing | Sensory receptors throughout the body register information about the external environment and send it up to the brain for processing. This is sensation. | Bottom-up = from the body up = sensation. |
| Top-down processing | Using your knowledge, experience, or context to understand and interpret sensory perceptions. This is perception. | Top-down = your brain's knowledge comes down = perception. |
| Reading scrambled words | You can read words with scrambled middle letters as long as the first and last letters are in the right place, because the mind reads the word as a whole, not every letter by itself. An example of top-down processing. |  |
| The handwriting example | Being aware of squiggles like "15" in a messy note is bottom-up (sensation). Knowing what the squiggles mean is top-down (perception). |  |

## Thresholds

> The absolute threshold is the weakest stimulus you can detect half the time. The difference threshold is the smallest change you notice half the time. Weber's law says that change is a constant percentage, not a constant amount.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute threshold | The weakest amount of a stimulus that a person can detect 50% of the time: the moment when you notice something. |  |
| Absolute threshold examples | Hearing a watch ticking 20 feet away; tasting one teaspoon of salt dissolved in two gallons of water; the volume of the music in your car. |  |
| Difference threshold (just noticeable difference) | The minimum amount something needs to change before a person notices the change 50% of the time. | JND = just noticeable difference. |
| JND example | Car music on level 5: you probably can't notice a change to 6, but you probably can notice a change from 5 to 10. |  |
| Weber's law | For an average person to perceive a difference, two stimuli must differ by a constant percentage, not a constant amount. | Weber = percentage. |
| Weber's law examples | Add 1 ounce to a 10-ounce weight and you will detect the difference; add 1 ounce to a 100-ounce weight and you likely won't. Weight must change by 2% to notice a difference. |  |

## Extrasensory perception

> ESP claims include telepathy, clairvoyance and precognition. There is no scientific basis for it; psychics do no better than guesses, and they seem accurate because people remember the hits and forget the misses.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Extrasensory perception (ESP) | The claim of perceiving without the senses: telepathy, clairvoyance, and precognition. There is no scientific basis for it. |  |
| Telepathy | Mind-to-mind communication. | Tele-path = sending a message down a path to another mind. |
| Clairvoyance | Perceiving events you didn't actually witness, like knowing where a body is buried or how someone was murdered. | Clair-voyance = "clear seeing" of something you weren't there for. |
| Precognition | Perceiving future events. | Pre = before it happens. |
| Psychics and the police | Psychic visions given to police are no more accurate than guesses made by others. |  |
| Why psychics seem accurate | Psychics make so many predictions that one of them is bound to be correct at some point, and people focus on the 1 or 2 correct predictions and forget most of the incorrect ones. |  |
