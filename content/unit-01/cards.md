# Deck 1: The Neuron

> Parts of a neuron, how it fires, and two diseases that disrupt it.

## Parts of a neuron

> A neuron is the nervous system's building block, supported by glial cells. A signal comes in through the dendrites, the soma decides whether to fire, the impulse travels down the axon (sped up by the myelin sheath), and vesicles in the terminal buttons release chemicals across the synapse.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Neuron | A nerve cell; the basic building block of the brain and nervous system. Hundreds of billions form networks throughout the body. |  |
| Glial cells (glia) | Support cells in the brain that nourish and protect neurons. Your slides say there are about 50x more of them than neurons. | "Glia" = glue: they hold the system together. |
| Dendrites | Branch-like structures extending from the cell body. Their receptors receive chemical signals from other neurons. | Dendrites = Detect. |
| Soma | The cell body. Holds the nucleus, keeps the cell alive, and decides when the neuron will fire. |  |
| Axon | The long pathway an electrical impulse travels down when the neuron fires. | Axon = Away from the cell body. |
| Myelin sheath | Fatty coating that protects the axon and speeds up the electrical signal. | Like insulation on a wire. |
| Terminal branches | The frayed ends of the axon. Each one ends in a terminal button. |  |
| Terminal buttons | Knobs at the ends of the terminal branches that hold the vesicles. |  |
| Vesicles | Tiny sacs in the terminal buttons that store neurotransmitters. |  |
| Synapse | The gap between two neurons (also called the synaptic cleft or synaptic gap). Neurons never actually touch. |  |

## Firing a neuron

> A neuron at resting potential fires only if stimulation reaches threshold, and then the action potential is all-or-none: it fires fully or not at all. Afterward it can't fire during the refractory period, and leftover neurotransmitters are taken back up (reuptake).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Resting potential | The neuron is not firing; its dendrites are waiting to receive neurotransmitters. |  |
| Threshold | The level of stimulation the dendrites must receive before the neuron will fire. |  |
| Action potential | The electrical signal that the soma starts once threshold is reached; it travels down the axon. |  |
| All-or-none principle | A neuron either fires completely or not at all. It never partially fires, and a stronger stimulus does not make a stronger firing. | Like a gun: you either pull the trigger or you don't. |
| Reuptake | Leftover neurotransmitters in the synapse get reabsorbed by the neuron that released them. |  |
| Refractory period | The recovery time after firing when the neuron cannot fire again, no matter how many neurotransmitters it receives. | Like a toilet: you have to wait to flush again. |

## Diseases

> Two diseases disrupt neurons in different ways. Multiple sclerosis breaks down the myelin sheath; myasthenia gravis blocks the muscles' receptors for acetylcholine. Both cause weakness, so the cause is what tells them apart.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple sclerosis (MS) | Disease caused by deterioration of the myelin sheath (demyelination). Causes trouble with movement and walking. | MS = Myelin Sheath. |
| Myasthenia gravis | Autoimmune disorder where antibodies block or destroy the muscles' receptor sites for acetylcholine, causing muscle weakness. |  |

# Deck 2: Neurotransmitters

> Excitatory vs. inhibitory, and what each chemical does — plus what too much or too little looks like.

## Basics

> Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that cross the synapse. Excitatory ones make the next neuron more likely to fire; inhibitory ones make it less likely.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Neurotransmitter | A chemical messenger released into the synapse that carries a signal from one neuron to the next. |  |
| Excitatory | Describes a neurotransmitter that makes the next neuron more likely to fire. | Excite = Go. |
| Inhibitory | Describes a neurotransmitter that keeps the next neuron from firing. | Inhibit = Stop. |

## The chemicals

> Learn each neurotransmitter with its job and what goes wrong. Acetylcholine: movement and memory. Dopamine: reward and movement. Serotonin: mood (SSRIs keep it in the synapse). Norepinephrine: alertness. GABA: the main brake. Glutamate: the main accelerator. Endorphins: natural painkillers. Substance P: pain signals.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine (ACh) | Mainly controls movement (muscle action) and also plays a role in memory. Found in both the central and peripheral nervous systems. Too little is linked to Alzheimer's; too much causes muscle spasms; blocked receptors cause paralysis. | ACh = ACTion (muscles) + Alzheimer's. |
| Dopamine | The "pleasure chemical": reward pathway, anticipating something good, movement, alertness, and motor coordination. Low levels are linked to Parkinson's and addictive behaviors; high levels are linked to schizophrenia. | Low = Parkinson's shakes. High = schizophrenia. |
| Serotonin | Affects mood, emotion, appetite, and sleep. High levels mean more happiness; low levels are linked to depression, anger-control problems, and OCD. |  |
| SSRI | Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor. An antidepressant that blocks reuptake so serotonin stays in the synapse longer. |  |
| Norepinephrine | Acts as both a neurotransmitter and a hormone (noradrenaline). Drives the sympathetic fight-or-flight response, plus focus, attention, and quick decisions. High levels are linked to developing fears; low levels to depression and ADHD. |  |
| GABA | Gamma-aminobutyric acid: the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. A natural tranquilizer that calms the nervous system. Too little is linked to anxiety disorders; too much causes sleepiness. | GABA = Gotta Always Be Asleep. |
| Glutamate | The major excitatory neurotransmitter. Involved in thinking, memory, and learning. Too much is linked to high anxiety, OCD, and migraines. | Glutamate = Go. |
| Endorphins | The brain's natural painkillers, released by the pituitary gland. Relieve pain and stress; example: runner's high. | "Endogenous morphine." |
| Substance P | Involved in pain regulation (sending pain signals). Levels rise with stress, heat, and painful stimuli. | P = Pain. |

# Deck 3: Nervous & Endocrine Systems

> How the nervous system is divided, the three neuron types, and the hormones you need to know.

## Functions & neurons

> The nervous system takes in information, processes it and responds. Sensory (afferent) neurons carry messages in, motor (efferent) neurons carry commands out, and interneurons connect them inside the brain and spinal cord.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Three functions of the nervous system | Sensory input (gather info from the senses), integration (the brain processes it and decides), and motor output (signals go to muscles and glands). |  |
| Sensory (afferent) neurons | Carry messages from sensory receptors in to the brain and spinal cord. | Afferent = Arrive. |
| Motor (efferent) neurons | Carry signals from the brain and spinal cord out to muscles and organs. | Efferent = Exit. |
| Interneurons | Relay neurons in the brain and spinal cord that connect sensory and motor neurons and pass information between them. | Inter = in between. |

## Divisions

> The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord; everything else is the peripheral nervous system. The PNS splits into somatic (voluntary movement) and autonomic (automatic) systems, and the autonomic splits into sympathetic (fight or flight) and parasympathetic (rest and digest). Reflexes run through the spinal cord.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Central nervous system (CNS) | The brain and the spinal cord. |  |
| Spinal cord | The "highway" connecting the brain to the rest of the body; sends messages both ways. |  |
| Peripheral nervous system (PNS) | All the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord, connecting them to the rest of the body. |  |
| Nerves | Bundles of neurons that act like wires carrying messages to and from the brain and spinal cord. |  |
| Somatic nervous system | Part of the peripheral system that controls voluntary movements, like walking or picking up a pencil. | Soma = body you move on purpose. |
| Autonomic nervous system | Part of the peripheral system that controls involuntary actions like heartbeat and digestion. Split into sympathetic and parasympathetic. | Autonomic = automatic. |
| Sympathetic nervous system | Arouses the body: the fight, flight, or freeze response. | Sympathetic = Stressed. |
| Parasympathetic nervous system | Calms the body back down: rest and digest. | Parasympathetic = Peace (or a Parachute down). |
| Reflex | A simple, automatic response to a stimulus (like the knee-jerk), directed by the spinal cord instead of the brain, which makes it faster. |  |

## Endocrine system

> The endocrine system sends hormones through the blood, so it's slower than neurons but its effects last longer. The pituitary is the master gland. Know each hormone's job: adrenaline (emergency energy), ghrelin (hunger), leptin (fullness), testosterone, estrogen, oxytocin (bonding) and melatonin (sleep).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Endocrine system | The system of glands that releases and regulates hormones. |  |
| Hormones vs. neurotransmitters | Hormones travel through the bloodstream, so they act more slowly than neurotransmitters, but their effects last longer. |  |
| Pituitary gland | The "master gland." Regulates stress, growth, and reproduction and signals the other glands to release their hormones. |  |
| Adrenaline | Hormone from the adrenal glands (on top of the kidneys). Raises heart rate, blood pressure, and blood sugar for the fight-or-flight energy surge. |  |
| Ghrelin | Hunger hormone made in the stomach; tells the body you are hungry. | Ghrelin = Growling stomach. |
| Leptin | Hormone that tells the body you are full (satiety). Your slides say it is made in the small intestine. | Leptin = Leave the table. |
| Testosterone | Sex hormone made in the testes and ovaries; the hormone most responsible for sex drive. Men have more. Too much is linked to aggression. |  |
| Estrogen | Sex hormone important for reproduction; women have more of it. |  |
| Oxytocin | The "love" or social-bonding hormone. Made by the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary. Important in labor, mother–baby bonding, and bonding with friends and partners. Low levels are linked to postpartum depression. |  |
| Melatonin | Hormone from the pineal gland, released in response to darkness. Regulates circadian rhythm (sleep-wake cycle) and helps you sleep. |  |

# Deck 4: The Brain

> Old brain, limbic system, the four lobes (F-POT), lateralization, plasticity, and brain scans.

## Old brain

> The old brain keeps you alive and alert. The brainstem includes the medulla (breathing and heartbeat) and the pons (sleep-wake). The reticular formation controls arousal, the thalamus relays the senses (except smell), and the cerebellum coordinates balance and movement.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Brainstem | The medulla plus the pons; handles automatic survival functions. |  |
| Medulla | Base of the brainstem; controls life-sustaining functions like breathing and heartbeat. | Medulla = Most important (keeps you alive). |
| Pons | Connects the spinal cord to the brain; involved in automatic processes, especially the sleep-wake cycle. | Pons = bridge (French "pont"). |
| Reticular formation | Network in the brainstem (part of the pons, per your slides) that controls arousal and consciousness. Damage can cause a coma. |  |
| Thalamus | Sits on top of the brainstem; the brain's sensory relay station. All senses except smell get routed through it. | Thalamus = sensory switchboard. |
| Cerebellum | "Little brain" at the back. Handles fine motor control, coordination, posture, and balance, plus learned motor skills like riding a bike. Alcohol strongly affects it. | Cerebellum = balance (think "bell" ringing while you wobble). |

## Limbic system

> The limbic system handles emotion, memory and drives. The amygdala processes fear and emotion, the hippocampus forms memories, and the hypothalamus controls drives like hunger: the lateral hypothalamus turns hunger on and the ventromedial hypothalamus turns it off.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Limbic system | Brain structures involved in emotion and long-term memory: the amygdala, hippocampus, and hypothalamus. |  |
| Amygdala | Processes emotion, especially fear and aggression. Triggers fight-or-flight, helps read others' emotions, and helps store emotional memories. | Two almonds of fear. |
| Hippocampus | Helps store information into long-term memory. One of the first areas damaged by Alzheimer's. | A hippo never forgets. |
| Hypothalamus | Regulates the autonomic nervous system and monitors body temperature, thirst, hunger, and sexual responses. | The 4 Fs: feeding, fighting, fleeing, and mating. |
| Lateral hypothalamus (LH) | Part of the hypothalamus that triggers feelings of hunger. Damage → loss of hunger. | Lateral = Lunch. |
| Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) | Part of the hypothalamus that signals fullness. Damage → overeating. | Ventromedial = Very full. |

## Cerebral cortex

> The cortex has four lobes (F-POT). The frontal lobe plans and moves (prefrontal cortex, motor cortex, Broca's area for speech); Phineas Gage's injury showed its role in personality. The parietal lobe handles touch, the temporal lobe hearing and language (Wernicke's area), and the occipital lobe vision.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Cerebral cortex | The wrinkled outer layer of the brain, split into a left and right hemisphere. Its four lobes: frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal (F-POT). |  |
| Frontal lobe | Front of the brain; home of the prefrontal cortex, motor cortex, and Broca's area. |  |
| Prefrontal cortex | Front of the frontal lobe; executive functions — judgment, planning, reasoning, problem solving, moral judgment, and personality. |  |
| Motor cortex | Strip at the rear of the frontal lobe that controls voluntary movement. | Motor is in front of Sensory (somatosensory) — "M before S." |
| Broca's area | Left frontal lobe, next to the motor cortex; controls speech production. | Broca = broken speech. |
| Broca's aphasia | Difficulty producing speech; sentences are short and simple, often just a noun and a verb, but understanding is mostly fine. |  |
| Phineas Gage | Railroad worker who had an iron rod driven through his prefrontal cortex. His personality changed: irritable, profane, dishonest, poor emotional control. |  |
| Parietal lobe | Top of the brain; processes body sensations (touch, pressure, temperature, pain) and spatial orientation. |  |
| Somatosensory cortex | Strip in the parietal lobe, directly behind the motor cortex, that registers touch and body movement sensations. |  |
| Temporal lobe | Side of the brain, above the ears; hearing, language processing, and long-term memory storage. | Temporal = by your temples, near your ears. |
| Primary auditory cortex | Area in the temporal lobe where hearing is processed. |  |
| Wernicke's area | Temporal lobe area for comprehending written and spoken language. | Wernicke = words don't make sense. |
| Wernicke's aphasia | Difficulty understanding and expressing language; speech can flow but makes little sense. |  |
| Occipital lobe | Back of the brain; processes visual information. Has neurons that recognize lines, angles, shapes, and movement. Damage can cause blindness. | Occipital = Optical. |

## Hemispheres

> The corpus callosum connects the two hemispheres. Each hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body (contralateral control), and each specializes (lateralization): the left in language, the right in things like tone and context.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Corpus callosum | Large band of neural fibers connecting the left and right hemispheres so they can communicate. |  |
| Brain lateralization | The tendency for certain functions to be specialized to one side of the brain. |  |
| Contralateral control | Each side of the brain controls the opposite side of the body: right brain → left body, left brain → right body. |  |
| Left hemisphere | Speech, language, and comprehension; math calculations; time and sequencing; recognizing words, letters, and numbers; controls the right side of the body. | Left = Language and Logic. |
| Right hemisphere | Spatial ability; recognizing faces, places, and objects; perceiving and expressing emotion; tone and emphasis in speech; controls the left side of the body. |  |

## Change & damage

> The brain can reorganize itself after experience or damage (plasticity), most of all in childhood. New neurons grow (neurogenesis) in only a few areas.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Plasticity | The brain's ability to change — reorganizing after damage or building new pathways through experience. Example: in a blind Braille reader, touch takes over parts of the visual cortex. | Plastic = moldable. |
| Neurogenesis | The formation of new neurons. Only happens in two areas (one for memory, one for smell); usually a destroyed neuron cannot be replaced. |  |

## Brain scans

> Some scans show structure (CT, MRI) and others show activity (EEG, PET, fMRI). fMRI shows function through blood flow and is more precise than PET.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| EEG | Electroencephalogram: electrodes on the scalp record the brain's electrical activity (brain waves). Used to find seizures. | E = Electrical. |
| PET scan | Positron emission tomography: radioactive glucose is injected and the scan shows where it is being used — which regions are active. |  |
| CT scan | X-rays that show brain structure — the shape and position of structures. Helps find tumors. |  |
| MRI | Magnetic resonance imaging: strong magnetic pulses build a detailed picture of brain structure (tissue, tumors, clots). |  |
| fMRI | Functional MRI: shows brain function by measuring blood flow and oxygen use. More precise than PET. | f = function. |

# Deck 5: Vision

> The path light takes, rods and cones, color theories, Gestalt grouping, and depth cues.

## The eye

> Light passes the cornea and pupil (sized by the iris), the lens focuses it (accommodation), and it lands on the retina. There rods (dim light, no color) and cones (color, bright light) turn light into neural signals (transduction), which pass through bipolar and ganglion cells to the optic nerve.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Cornea | Clear, protective outer layer where light first enters the eye. |  |
| Iris | Colored muscle that surrounds the pupil and controls its size. Unique enough to identify you (iris scanners). |  |
| Pupil | Small adjustable opening that lets light through. |  |
| Lens | Transparent structure behind the pupil that changes shape to focus images on the retina. |  |
| Accommodation | The lens changing shape to focus on near or far objects. |  |
| Retina | Light-sensitive inner surface at the back of the eye containing rods and cones. Where transduction happens. |  |
| Transduction | Converting one form of energy (like light) into neural impulses the brain can understand. |  |
| Rods | Retina receptors for black-and-white vision; let you see in dim light. | Rods = Reduced light. |
| Cones | Retina receptors for color and fine detail; work poorly in dim light. | Cones = Color. |
| Dark adaptation | In darkness the pupils dilate to let more light reach the retina; it takes about 20 minutes. |  |
| Bipolar cells | Connect the rods and cones to the ganglion cells. |  |
| Ganglion cells | Cells whose axons bundle together to form the optic nerve. |  |
| Optic nerve | Carries neural impulses from the eye to the brain. |  |

## In the brain

> The primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe processes what the eyes send. Feature detectors there respond to edges, angles and movement, and the brain handles many parts of a scene at once (parallel processing).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Primary visual cortex | Where visual input is first processed; located in the occipital lobe. |  |
| Feature detectors | Neurons in the visual cortex that respond to specific features like shape, angle, or movement. |  |
| Parallel processing | Processing many aspects of a stimulus (color, motion, form, depth) at the same time, then combining them into one image. |  |

## Color

> Two theories explain color. Trichromatic theory: red, green and blue cones, which explains color blindness. Opponent-process theory: paired opposites, which explains afterimages. Both are right at different stages.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Trichromatic theory | The retina has three types of color receptors — red, green, and blue — that combine to produce any color. Explains color at the retina level. | Tri = three cones. |
| Color blindness | Inability to see colors normally, usually from impaired red or green cones (rarely blue). |  |
| Opponent-process theory | Color is processed by opposing pairs — red/green, yellow/blue, white/black — and a cell can signal only one of each pair at a time. Explains color in the brain and afterimages. |  |
| Afterimage | A visual image that persists after the stimulus is removed — e.g., stare at green, then see red on a white wall. |  |
| Which color theory is right? | Both. Trichromatic explains how the retina detects color; opponent-process explains how the brain processes it. |  |

## Gestalt

> Gestalt means the brain organizes parts into a whole. It groups things that are close (proximity) or alike (similarity), fills in gaps (closure), and separates a figure from its background (figure-ground).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Gestalt | An organized whole. Gestalt psychologists said we combine pieces of information into meaningful wholes (you see a face, not a set of lines). |  |
| Proximity | Grouping objects that are close together (how you see separate words on a page). |  |
| Similarity | Grouping objects that look alike. |  |
| Closure | Filling in gaps to see a complete, closed shape. |  |
| Figure-ground | Organizing what you see into objects (figures) that stand out from their surroundings (ground). |  |

## Depth

> Depth perception uses binocular cues, which need both eyes (retinal disparity), and monocular cues, which work with one eye: relative size, interposition, linear perspective and relative clarity.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Depth perception | The ability to judge how far away an object is. |  |
| Binocular cues | Depth cues that need both eyes. | Bi = two eyes. |
| Retinal disparity | A binocular cue: because your eyes are about 2.5 inches apart, each retina gets a slightly different image; the bigger the difference, the closer the object. |  |
| Monocular cues | Depth cues that work with just one eye; used for farther distances. | Mono = one eye. |
| Relative size | Monocular cue: if two objects are assumed to be the same size, the one that looks smaller seems farther away. |  |
| Interposition | Monocular cue: an object that blocks part of another is seen as closer. |  |
| Linear perspective | Monocular cue: parallel lines seem to converge in the distance (train tracks). |  |
| Relative clarity | Monocular cue: sharp, clear objects seem closer; hazy ones seem farther away. |  |

# Deck 6: Hearing, Smell, Taste, Touch & more

> Sound waves, hearing loss, balance, smell, taste, pain, and unusual perception.

## Hearing

> Sound waves have amplitude (loudness) and frequency (pitch). Sound moves through the eardrum and ossicles to the cochlea. Damage to the eardrum or ossicles causes conduction hearing loss, often fixable; damage to the cochlea's hair cells causes sensorineural loss, which a cochlear implant can help.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Audition | The sense of hearing. |  |
| Amplitude | The height of a sound wave; determines loudness. High amplitude = loud. | Amplitude = Amplifier (volume). |
| Frequency | Number of wavelengths passing a point in a given time; determines pitch. High frequency = high pitch; low frequency = bass. |  |
| Sound localization | Telling where a sound comes from: the nearer ear hears it slightly louder and slightly sooner. |  |
| Eardrum | Membrane between the outer and middle ear that vibrates when sound waves hit it. (Standard ear part; check the ear diagram slide.) |  |
| Ossicles | Three tiny bones in the middle ear (hammer, anvil, stirrup) that pass vibrations to the cochlea. (Check the ear diagram slide.) |  |
| Cochlea | Snail-shaped, fluid-filled tube in the inner ear whose hair cells turn vibrations into neural signals. (Check the ear diagram slide.) |  |
| Conduction hearing loss | Damage to the parts that conduct sound through the outer and middle ear (eardrum, ossicles, auditory canal). Can be fixed with surgery. | Conduction = Correctable. |
| Sensorineural hearing loss | Nerve deafness: damage to the cochlea's hair cells or the auditory nerve (inner ear). Cannot be reversed. |  |
| Cochlear implant | Device wired into the cochlea that turns sounds into electrical signals sent up the auditory nerve. Used for sensorineural hearing loss. |  |

## Body position

> Two senses track your body. The vestibular sense, in the inner ear, handles balance; kinesthesis tracks the position and movement of your body parts.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Vestibular sense | Senses the head's position and movement (balance) using fluid in the semicircular canals and vestibular sacs. Signals go to the cerebellum. The fluid keeps moving after you stop, which causes dizziness. | Vestibular = balance in the ear. |
| Kinesthesis | Senses the position and movement of individual body parts using sensors in muscles, tendons, and joints; feeds the cerebellum. | Kinesthesis = kinetic body parts. |

## Smell & taste

> Smell (olfaction) is the one sense that skips the thalamus and connects straight to emotion and memory areas. Taste (gustation) has five basic tastes. Supertasters feel tastes more intensely, and smell strongly affects taste (sensory interaction).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Olfaction | The sense of smell. Receptors at the top of the nasal cavity detect inhaled particles. Smell skips the thalamus and goes to areas connected to the limbic system. |  |
| Gustation | The sense of taste. The tongue has about 200 taste buds, each with 50–100 receptors; taste buds regenerate about every two weeks. |  |
| Five basic tastes | Sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami (savory/protein). |  |
| Supertasters | People with more taste buds than average, who experience tastes and pain more intensely (e.g., jalapeños burn more). |  |
| Sensory interaction | One sense influencing another — like smell affecting taste (holding your nose to take bad medicine). |  |

## Touch & pain

> Touch includes several skin senses. Gate control theory says a 'gate' in the spinal cord can block pain signals or let them through. Phantom limb is feeling sensation in a missing limb.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Touch | A mix of four skin senses: pressure, warmth, cold, and pain. |  |
| Gate control theory | The spinal cord has a neurological "gate" that either blocks pain signals or lets them through to the brain. Distraction can close the gate. |  |
| Phantom limb | Feeling that an amputated limb is still there, because the sensory cortex area for that limb still has activity. |  |

## Unusual perception

> Two unusual conditions: synesthesia, where one sense triggers another, and prosopagnosia, face blindness.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Synesthesia | When one sense triggers another — like hearing music or seeing a number and experiencing a color. |  |
| Prosopagnosia | Face blindness: the inability to recognize faces. |  |

# Deck 7: Sleep, Consciousness & Drugs

> Everything on the Sleep, Consciousness, and Drugs slides: sleep, circadian rhythm, sleep loss, sleep disorders, how drugs work, the four drug categories, and addiction.

## Sleep & consciousness

> Sleep is an altered state of consciousness, not dormancy: the brain stays active, and everyone dreams every night. Sleep moves through stages in a repeating cycle.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep | An altered state of consciousness. It is not a period of dormancy where your body and brain shut off: even when you are sound asleep, your brain is still active. |  |
| Dreaming | Everyone dreams every night. |  |
| Sleep stages | Sleep has four stages: NREM 1 (Stage 1), NREM 2 (Stage 2), NREM 3 (Stage 3), and REM sleep. | Three NREM stages, then REM. |

## Circadian rhythm

> The circadian rhythm is the body's 24-hour biological clock. Body temperature rises through the day, dips in the afternoon and is lowest at night. Teens and young adults tend to be evening people, older adults morning people, and artificial light can shift the clock.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Circadian rhythm | The biological clock: regular bodily rhythms that occur on a 24-hour cycle. The body is roughly synchronized with the 24-hour cycle of day and night. |  |
| Body temperature cycle | Body temperature increases throughout the day, dips in the afternoon, and is lowest at night. |  |
| Teens and young adults | Most are evening-energized, with performance improving across the day. |  |
| Older adults | Most are morning people, with energy declining as the day goes on. |  |
| Artificial light | Light from cell phones, lamps, and other devices can interfere with circadian rhythms. |  |

## Effects of sleep loss

> Sleep loss hurts the whole body: weaker focus and memory, a weaker immune system, more hunger (ghrelin up, leptin down) and more fat cells, heart risks, and effects on muscles and joints.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep loss: brain | Diminished attentional focus and memory consolidation, and increased risk of depression. |  |
| Sleep loss: immune system | Suppression of immune cell production and increased risk of viral infections, such as colds. |  |
| Sleep loss: stomach and fat cells | Increased hunger-arousing ghrelin and decreased hunger-suppressing leptin, plus increased fat cell production and greater risk of obesity. | Ghrelin = growling stomach goes up; Leptin = leave the table goes down. |
| Sleep loss: heart | Increased risk of high blood pressure. |  |
| Sleep loss: muscles and joints | Muscles: reduced strength and slower reaction time and motor learning. Joints: increased inflammation and arthritis. |  |

## Sleep disorders

> Know each disorder by its key sign: insomnia (trouble sleeping), sleep apnea (breathing stops), night terrors (terror in Stage 3, not remembered), sleepwalking, and narcolepsy (suddenly falling asleep).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Insomnia | Can't sleep. |  |
| Sleep apnea | You stop breathing in your sleep. | Apnea = no air. |
| Night terrors | Not nightmares: intense fear or terror where you wake up screaming or crying, with no recollection the following morning. Happen in Stage 3. |  |
| Sleepwalking | A sleep disorder: walking while asleep. |  |
| Narcolepsy | You fall asleep spontaneously. | Narco = sleep attack. |

## How drugs work

> Psychoactive drugs change how neurons communicate. Agonists mimic a neurotransmitter, antagonists block it, and reuptake inhibitors keep it in the synapse longer. The drugs fall into four categories: depressants, stimulants, hallucinogens and opioids.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Psychoactive drugs | Chemical substances that alter perceptions and mood. They affect the synapses of neurons and the functioning of neurotransmitters. |  |
| Agonist | A drug that fits into receptors and mimics a neurotransmitter, increasing its effects. Example: opiates mimic and increase the effects of endorphins. | Agonist = Acts like it. |
| Antagonist | A drug that blocks the effects of a neurotransmitter. Example from your slides: cocaine blocks the reuptake of dopamine. | Antagonist = Against it. |
| Reuptake inhibitor | A drug that blocks reuptake, so the neurotransmitter isn't reabsorbed. Your slides' example: cocaine blocks the reuptake of dopamine. |  |
| Four categories of psychoactive drugs | Depressants, stimulants, hallucinogens, and opioids. |  |

## Depressants

> Depressants slow neural activity. Alcohol boosts GABA and reduces glutamate; it only seems like a stimulant because of how people act. Barbiturates and benzodiazepines are also depressants.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Depressants | Drugs that slow you down by reducing neural activity. Examples: alcohol, barbiturates, sedatives, benzodiazepines. | Depress = press down. |
| Alcohol | A depressant that calms the nervous system. It's perceived as a stimulant because people do crazy things, but it intensifies the effects of GABA (sluggish movements, slurred speech) and reduces glutamate (slower reactions, impaired judgment). |  |
| Barbiturates / sedatives | Also called "downers." Used to treat insomnia; effects are similar to alcohol. They mess with sleep patterns and cause dependence. They increase GABA, which has a calming effect. |  |
| Benzodiazepines | Valium and Xanax. Anti-anxiety drugs that calm the nervous system. They depress heartbeat and breathing. |  |

## Stimulants

> Stimulants speed the body up. Caffeine and nicotine are mild; cocaine blocks dopamine reuptake, and amphetamines mimic adrenaline. The strong ones bring a high followed by a crash and cravings.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulants | Drugs that excite the central nervous system. Examples: caffeine, nicotine, cocaine, amphetamines, meth. |  |
| Caffeine | Naturally occurring (coffee, tea, chocolate); increases alertness. In high doses it can cause anxiety, heart palpitations, and insomnia. |  |
| Nicotine | A highly addictive stimulant. Depending on the amount of time smoked, it can have stimulating or sedative effects. Withdrawal symptoms: irritability, headaches, nervousness. |  |
| Cocaine | Blocks reabsorption of dopamine to produce increased alertness, euphoria, and motivation. Followed by a huge crash with anxiety, depression, and strong cravings. |  |
| Amphetamines | Mimic epinephrine/adrenaline. Increase alertness and feelings of well-being and give a sense of euphoria, followed by a huge crash. Leads to a cycle of addiction. Meth and ecstasy/MDMA are forms. |  |

## Hallucinogens

> Hallucinogens distort perception. LSD can cause psychosis-like states; THC causes mild hallucinations, relaxation and time distortion; ecstasy (MDMA) is both a stimulant and a hallucinogen and can damage serotonin neurons.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Hallucinogens | Substances that distort visual and auditory perception. Examples: LSD, marijuana. |  |
| LSD | Produces hallucinations and delusions, like a psychotic state. Can result in psychosis, memory loss, paranoia, panic attacks, nightmares, and aggression. |  |
| THC | The active ingredient in marijuana. Can produce mild hallucinations, euphoria, relaxation, a sense of wellbeing, and a distortion of time. |  |
| Ecstasy (MDMA) | Considered both a stimulant and a hallucinogen. Produces a euphoric high and can damage serotonin-producing neurons, resulting in a permanent deflation of mood and impairment of memory. |  |

## Opioids

> Opioids such as heroin act as agonists for endorphins, relieving pain. With continued use the brain makes less of its own dopamine and endorphins, leading to dependence, tolerance and withdrawal.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Opioids | Drugs derived from the opium poppy that lessen pain and anxiety. Examples: heroin, codeine, morphine, methadone, OxyContin, oxycodone. |  |
| How opioids work | They act as agonists for endorphins, which reduce pain and increase positive feelings. With continued use, the brain stops producing dopamine and endorphins. |  |
| Results of opioid use | Dependence, tolerance, withdrawal, and physiological and psychological addiction. |  |

## Addiction

> Substance use doesn't interfere with daily life; substance abuse does. Tolerance means needing more for the same effect, withdrawal is the distress after stopping, and addiction is compulsive use despite harm.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Substance use | Using a substance, but it doesn't interfere with daily functioning. |  |
| Substance abuse | A pattern of use that inhibits the ability to function, often puts the user in danger, and results in legal difficulties. |  |
| Tolerance | A need for larger amounts of the drug to experience the same effects. |  |
| Addiction | The user craves the drug and continues to use it despite adverse consequences. Users want the drug more than they like the drug. Includes withdrawal symptoms. | Want it more than you like it. |
| Withdrawal | The discomfort and distress that follow discontinuing an addictive drug. Can be physiological and/or psychological. |  |

## Substance use disorder

> The DSM-5 lists 11 criteria for substance use disorder, grouped as losing control, life problems, and risk and the body. Severity depends on how many criteria a person meets: mild, moderate or severe.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Substance use disorder | A disorder characterized by continued substance craving and use despite significant life disruption and/or physical risk. Diagnosed with 11 DSM-5 criteria. |  |
| Severity levels | Per your slide: mild = 3 criteria met, moderate = 4 or 5, severe = 6 or more. |  |
| Criteria: losing control | Taking the substance in larger amounts or for longer than you meant to; wanting to cut down or stop but not managing to; spending a lot of time getting, using, or recovering from it; cravings and urges to use it. |  |
| Criteria: life problems | Not doing what you should at work, home, or school; continuing even when it causes relationship problems; giving up important social, occupational, or recreational activities. |  |
| Criteria: risk and the body | Using again and again even when it puts you in danger; continuing even when you know it caused or worsened a physical or psychological problem; tolerance; withdrawal symptoms relieved by taking more. |  |

## Split-brain patients

> When the corpus callosum is cut, the hemispheres can't share information. Each visual field goes to the opposite hemisphere (crossing at the optic chiasm). Something shown to the right visual field reaches the left hemisphere, which can name it; something shown to the left visual field reaches the right hemisphere, which can't speak but can point with the left hand.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Split-brain patient | Someone whose corpus callosum has been surgically cut, usually to stop severe seizures from spreading. The two hemispheres can no longer share information. |  |
| Left visual field (LVF) | Everything to the left of where you are looking, in both eyes. It is received by the right half of each eye and sent to the right hemisphere. | Visual fields cross: left field → right brain. |
| Right visual field (RVF) | Everything to the right of where you are looking, in both eyes. It is received by the left half of each eye and sent to the left hemisphere, the language side. | Right field → left brain → can say it. |
| Optic chiasm | The point where the optic nerves cross, so each visual field goes to the opposite hemisphere. | Chiasm looks like an X, the crossing point. |
| Object flashed to the right visual field | Reaches only the left hemisphere. The patient can say the word and describe it, and can point to it or draw it with the right hand, but not the left. |  |
| Object flashed to the left visual field | Reaches only the right hemisphere. The patient says they saw "nothing," but can point to it or draw it with the left hand. | Left field → left hand, no words. |

## Hemisphere styles

> Split-brain research showed each hemisphere's style: the left focuses on details, words and logic; the right on the big picture, tone and intuition.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Trees vs. forest | The left hemisphere focuses on details and pieces (the "trees"). The right hemisphere sees the big picture and wholes, including the self (the "forest"). |  |
| Words vs. tone | In language, the left hemisphere handles words and their literal definitions. The right hemisphere handles tone, inflection, and context. |  |
| Logic vs. intuition | Left: thoughts, logic, linear and literal thinking, calculation. Right: feelings, intuition, inferences and associations, perception. |  |
