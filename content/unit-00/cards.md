# Deck 1: Perspectives in Psychology

> The 7 psychological perspectives from the 0.3 slides, what each one focuses on, and how to apply them to a scenario.

## What perspectives are

> A perspective is a lens for explaining behavior. Psychology uses seven of them, and the same behavior can be explained through several at once. This section names the idea; the next one goes through each lens.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Psychological perspectives | The different approaches psychologists use to explain human behavior and thought processes, like viewing a situation through a different set of lenses. There are 7. | Perspective = a pair of glasses. |
| The 7 perspectives | Psychodynamic, cognitive, behavioral, humanistic, biological, evolutionary, and sociocultural. | Your slide's hand: thumb = psychodynamic; fingers = cognitive, behavioral, humanism, biological; palm = sociocultural; wrist = evolutionary. |

## The seven perspectives

> Learn each perspective by its key word. Psychodynamic: the unconscious and childhood. Cognitive: thoughts. Behavioral: learning and rewards. Humanistic: potential and free will. Biological: genes, brain chemistry and hormones. Evolutionary: survival and reproduction. Sociocultural: culture.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Psychodynamic perspective | Behavior is influenced by your unconscious mind and past experiences, especially those from childhood. Based heavily on the ideas of Freud. | Iceberg: most of the mind is hidden below the surface. |
| Cognitive perspective | How our internal thought processes shape our behavior. Emphasizes memory, problem solving, intelligence, and perception. | Cognitive = thinking. |
| Behavioral perspective | How our behaviors have been influenced by our learned experiences. We often repeat behaviors that are reinforced by positive rewards. | The dog that jumps through a hoop for a treat. |
| Humanistic perspective | How humans can achieve their full potential, emphasizing free will, self-efficacy, and self-actualization. Also emphasizes relationships, positivity, and maximizing well-being. | Humanistic = hopeful, reaching your potential. |
| Biological perspective | The importance of genetics, brain chemistry, hormones, and neurotransmitters (like dopamine and serotonin) in influencing behavior and thinking. | Bio = body: genes, brain, chemicals. |
| Evolutionary perspective | Human behavior is a result of evolutionary processes that protect us, give us adaptive behaviors, and help us be more successful at reproducing. | What helped our ancestors survive. |
| Sociocultural perspective | Humans behave and act differently based on the influences of their culture or way of life. | Socio = society; culture = way of life. |

## Applying perspectives

> Test questions describe a behavior and ask which perspective explains it. Look for the key word in each explanation (unconscious, thoughts, rewards, potential, chemistry, ancestors, culture) and match it to its perspective, as in the Suzanne example.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Applying a perspective | To analyze a behavior from a perspective, explain it using what that perspective focuses on. The same behavior can be explained by more than one perspective. |  |
| Slide example: Suzanne | Suzanne loves romantic comedies. Your slide's sociocultural explanation: in her culture it's popular for women to enjoy romantic movies, and going gives her something to do with friends and an easy topic of conversation. |  |
| Perspective keywords | Unconscious, childhood, Freud → psychodynamic. Thoughts, memory, problem solving → cognitive. Rewards, learning → behavioral. Potential, free will, positivity → humanistic. Genes, hormones, neurotransmitters → biological. Ancestors, survival, reproduction → evolutionary. Culture, social norms → sociocultural. |  |

# Deck 2: Research Methods

> Everything on the Unit 0 Research Methods slides: why intuition fails, the scientific method, operational definitions, descriptive and correlational research, experiments, problems with research, and ethics.

## Why psychology needs research

> Psychology relies on research because intuition is unreliable. Three biases distort it: hindsight bias (I knew it all along), overconfidence (thinking you know more than you do), and misunderstanding probability (seeing patterns in random events).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Intuition | Understanding without conscious reasoning. Your slides ask whether we can rely on it. We can't, which is why psychology uses research. |  |
| Why intuition fails | Your slides: "Psychology exists because our intuition sucks," because of hindsight bias, misunderstanding of probability, and overconfidence. | HMO: Hindsight, Misunderstanding probability, Overconfidence. |
| Hindsight bias | The tendency to believe, after learning the outcome, that you would have foreseen it. | "I knew it all along!" |
| Overconfidence | The tendency to overestimate your knowledge and/or ability. |  |
| Misunderstanding of probability | People are bad at judging chance and very large numbers. Your class showed this with a coin toss and by guessing what could hold a million, a billion, and a trillion dollars. |  |

## The scientific method

> Research follows seven steps, starting with observation and ending with replication. A theory explains observations; a hypothesis is a testable prediction. Operational definitions say exactly how each variable is measured, which is what lets other researchers replicate a study.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| The scientific method (7 steps) | 1. Observe some aspect of the universe. 2. Develop a theory. 3. Use the theory to make a hypothesis. 4. Test the predictions with experiments, using operational definitions. 5. Evaluate the results. 6. Modify the theory in light of your findings. 7. Replicate. |  |
| Theory | A general idea that is consistent with what you have observed. Step 2 of the scientific method. |  |
| Hypothesis | An educated prediction or guess, made using a theory. Step 3 of the scientific method. Example from your slides: students are more likely to smile for their senior pictures if they have a friendly photographer. | Hypothesis = the testable prediction. |
| Operational definition | Exact specifications of how the research is conducted, including exactly how each variable will be measured. Needed in step 4. Your class did a Lego activity on this. | Operational = how it OPERATES in the study. |
| Why operational definitions matter | Research cannot be replicated without operational definitions. In the senior-picture example, both "smiling" and "friendliness" need one, because people disagree about how many of the students in the yearbook photos are smiling. |  |
| Replicate | Doing the research a second time to ensure the reliability of the results. Step 7 of the scientific method. | Replicate = repeat. |

## Types of research

> There are three kinds of research. Descriptive research describes behavior, correlational research finds relationships, and experimental research tests cause and effect. Only experiments can show cause.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Three types of research | Descriptive, correlational, and experimental. |  |
| Descriptive research | Describes behaviors, often using case studies, surveys, or naturalistic observations. |  |
| Correlational research | Associates different factors or variables: it tells us about the relationship between two variables. |  |
| Experimental research | Manipulates variables to establish cause-and-effect relationships. Of the three types, only experiments can show cause and effect. |  |

## Descriptive methods

> The three descriptive methods each trade something off. A case study goes deep on one person but may not apply to others. Naturalistic observation sees real behavior but people may notice the observer. A survey collects a lot of data fast but depends on honest self-reports.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Case study | A descriptive method that examines one individual or group in depth. |  |
| Case study: pros and cons | Advantages: in-depth information about a limited number of subjects; illustrates general principles that can be applied to others. Disadvantages: misleading if the individual is atypical or underrepresented; can overgeneralize the results. |  |
| Naturalistic observation | A descriptive technique that observes and records behavior in naturally occurring situations, without trying to manipulate or control the situation. |  |
| Naturalistic observation: pros and cons | Advantage: non-invasive, so subjects are more likely to show honest behavior in a natural setting. Disadvantages: can be time consuming; subjects may notice the observer and act differently; the observer may misinterpret the subjects' actions. |  |
| Survey | A descriptive technique used to get the self-reported attitudes and behaviors of a particular group, usually by questioning a representative random sample of the group. |  |
| Survey: pros and cons | Advantages: collects a large amount of data in a short time; flexible (online, in person, etc.). Disadvantages: poorly worded questions can skew responses; participants may try to please the researcher; people may lie to look better or have mistaken memories; can have a poor response rate. |  |

## Correlation

> A correlation shows how two variables move together: positive (same direction), negative (opposite directions) or zero. The coefficient's closeness to +1 or −1 shows strength; the sign only shows direction. Two rules to remember: positive doesn't mean good, and correlation doesn't prove causation.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Correlation | Research that tells us about the relationship between two variables (a "co-relation"). |  |
| Positive correlation | Two variables increase or decrease together. Examples from your slides: as sleep increases, memory increases; as salary decreases, spending also decreases; as hourly pay increases, employee morale increases. | Same direction = positive. |
| Negative correlation | When one variable increases, the other decreases. Examples from your slides: as absences increase, grades decrease; as hours of sleep decrease, levels of stress increase. | Opposite directions = negative. |
| Zero (no) correlation | No relationship between the two variables. On a scatterplot, the dots are scattered with no pattern. |  |
| Correlation coefficient | A number from -1.0 to +1.0. The sign shows the direction. The closer to +1 or -1, the stronger the relationship; 0 means no correlation. Your slide's chart: 1 perfect, 0.9 high, 0.5 low, 0 none, and the same on the negative side. |  |
| Positive isn't "good" | Positive doesn't mean "good" and negative doesn't mean "bad." The sign only describes the direction of the relationship. |  |
| Correlation does not equal causation | Two things being related doesn't mean one causes the other. Your slides' example: ice cream sales and violent crime are correlated, but ice cream doesn't turn people into criminals. Ice cream and sunburn are correlated because hot, sunny summer weather causes both. | A third factor (like summer weather) can cause both. |

## Experiments

> An experiment studies a sample drawn from a population, ideally a random, representative one. The experimental group gets the independent variable (what the researcher changes), the control group doesn't, and the dependent variable is what gets measured. A confounding variable is anything else that could explain the result.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Population | The group that the research is meant to apply to. |  |
| Sample | A variety of people from the population used as participants in the research. |  |
| Representative (random) sample | Participants chosen fairly to represent a population: everyone in the group being studied has an equal chance of being included. |  |
| Experimental group | Participants who receive the manipulated variable. Example from your slides: the group that takes the antidepressant. |  |
| Control group | Participants who do not receive the manipulated variable. Example from your slides: the group that doesn't take the antidepressant. Comparing the groups shows whether the medication really reduces depression. | Control = compare. |
| Independent variable (IV) | The factor that is manipulated or controlled by the researcher. Examples from your slides: amount of sleep (does sleep deprivation cause poor memory?); temperature (does room temperature affect test scores?). | IV = what I change. |
| Dependent variable (DV) | The factor that is measured or observed by the researcher. Examples from your slides: strength of memory; test scores. | The DV depends on the IV. DV = data. |
| Confounding variable | An outside influence that affects the results of the study. Example from your slides: when studying how exercise (IV) affects weight loss (DV), possible confounding variables are diet, amount of sleep, and genetic issues. |  |

## Problems with research

> Many things can distort results: expectations (placebo effect), being watched (Hawthorne effect), wanting to look good (social desirability bias), unrepresentative samples (sampling bias), and how questions are worded. Learn to name each problem from a short scenario.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Problems with research (all 11) | Placebo effect, Hawthorne effect, Barnum effect, confounding variables, hindsight bias, overconfidence, gambler's fallacy, social desirability bias, self-report bias, sampling bias, and wording effects (framing). |  |
| Placebo effect | Experimental results caused by expectations alone. Example: participants act or claim to feel a certain way because they think they received a drug that would cause that reaction. |  |
| Hawthorne effect | Participants changing their behavior because they know they are being observed. | Hawthorne = "they're watching." |
| Barnum effect | Believing that personality descriptions apply specifically to you (more than to other people), even though the description is filled with information that applies to everyone. Example: your horoscope. |  |
| Gambler's fallacy | The tendency to overestimate the occurrence of patterns in random data. |  |
| Social desirability bias | People giving answers that may be dishonest because they are seeking approval and/or want to highlight their character strengths. | Answering to look good. |
| Self-report bias | Participants try to affect the outcome of the research and/or are unrealistic about themselves. They may try to please the researcher or even purposely challenge the researcher's hypothesis. |  |
| Sampling bias | Researchers targeting participants to make their hypothesis more likely to be proven, or failing to gather a representative sample of the population, leading to skewed results. |  |
| Wording effects (framing) | How questions are worded can skew responses. |  |

## Ethics

> APA guidelines protect participants: participation is voluntary, people give informed consent before and are debriefed after, information stays confidential, there's no long-term harm, animals are treated humanely, and an institution must approve the study first.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Why ethics guidelines exist | Many famous psychology experiments were highly unethical and cannot be replicated. The American Psychological Association (APA) set principles to protect everyone involved in research. |  |
| APA ethical guidelines | Voluntary participation, confidentiality, informed consent, debriefing, no long-term physical or psychological harm, animal participants must be well cared for, and approval from a research institution. |  |
| Voluntary participation | People choose for themselves whether to take part in a study. |  |
| Confidentiality | Participants' information is kept private. |  |
| Informed consent | Giving potential participants enough information about the study to let them choose whether or not to participate. | Informed consent = BEFORE. |
| Debriefing | The explanation after the experiment of what the study was about, including its purpose and any deception. | Debriefing = AFTER. |
| No long-term harm | Studies may not cause long-term physical or psychological harm. |  |
| Animal participants | All animal participants must be well cared for. |  |
| Institutional approval | Research needs approval from a research institution. |  |

# Deck 3: Statistics & Data

> Everything on the 0.11 Statistical Reasoning slides: histograms, mean/median/mode, percentile rank, range, standard deviation, skew, bimodal curves, and inferential statistics.

## Descriptive statistics

> Descriptive statistics organize and summarize data. A frequency distribution shows how often each score appears, and a histogram is the bar graph that displays it.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive statistics | Statistics that help researchers organize data meaningfully. |  |
| Frequency distribution | A count of how often each score or range of scores appears, like the tally table of ages of people entering a store on your slide. |  |
| Histogram | A bar graph that shows a frequency distribution. Useful because it displays data visually and makes patterns in a distribution quicker to spot. |  |

## Central tendency

> Three ways to find the center: the mean (average), the median (middle score in order) and the mode (most common score). Percentile rank tells what percentage of scores fall below yours.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Mean | The average. Add up all the numbers, then divide by how many numbers there are. Example from your slides: 2, 3, 4, 5, 5, 8 → mean = 4.5. |  |
| Median | The middle number when the numbers are in numerical order. If there are two middle numbers, average them. Example from your slides: 2, 4, 5, 6, 9 → median = 5. | Median = middle, like the median strip in a road. |
| Mode | The number that appears most frequently. Example from your slides: 1, 3, 6, 6, 8 → mode = 6. | Mode = most. |
| Percentile rank | The percentage of scores that are less than a given score. Your slides call it a fourth measure of central tendency. Example: in the 85th percentile on a psychology test, your score is higher than 85% of your peers. |  |

## Variation

> Variation is how spread out the scores are. The range is highest minus lowest; standard deviation shows how much scores vary around the mean. On a normal curve about 68% of scores fall within 1 standard deviation and about 95% within 2.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Measures of variation | Tell us how similar or different scores are. |  |
| Range | The difference between the highest and lowest scores: subtract the lowest from the highest. Example from your slides: 75, 78, 86, 92, 98, 100 → 100 − 75 = 25. Only a rough estimate of variation. |  |
| Standard deviation | How much scores vary around the mean: whether they are packed together or spread out. A more useful measure of variation than the range. | Small SD = packed together. Big SD = spread out. |
| Normal curve | A bell-shaped curve where most scores fall near the mean and fewer fall near either extreme. |  |
| The 68% and 95% rule | In a normal curve, about 68% of scores fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean, and about 95% within 2. Your slide's IQ example: mean 100, SD 15, so about 68% score 85–115 and about 95% score 70–130. |  |

## Skewed data

> An outlier can pull a distribution out of shape. In positive skew the tail points right; in negative skew it points left. The mean always follows the tail, while a symmetrical distribution has mean = median = mode.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Outlier | A score far away from the rest. One or two outliers can impact results, especially the mean. Your slide's example: a billionaire moving into a poor town could push the mean income to $1,000,000, so the median and mode give a fuller picture. |  |
| Skewed distribution | When an outlier disproportionately affects the mean, a few scores stretch away from the group like a tail. The skew is named for the direction of the tail. | Skew = where the tail goes. |
| Positive skew | The tail goes to the right, and the mean goes to the right too. Order: mean > median > mode. | Tail toward the bigger (positive) numbers = positive. |
| Negative skew | The tail goes to the left, and the mean goes to the left too. Order: mean < median < mode. |  |
| Symmetrical distribution | No skew: mean = median = mode. |  |
| The mean follows the tail | In a skewed distribution, the mean always moves in the direction of the tail. The mode stays at the peak. |  |
| Bimodal curve | A graph with two peaks ("bi" means two). Each peak is a local maximum: the highest values compared with the data points right around them. |  |

## Inferential statistics

> Inferential statistics ask whether results apply beyond the sample. Representative samples, bigger samples and more estimates are better; a meta-analysis combines many studies. A result is statistically significant when p ≤ .05, and effect size tells how strong the effect is.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Inferential statistics | Statistics that help determine whether results can be generalized to a larger population: confirming that results aren't just random fluctuations in the participants sampled, but true of a population. |  |
| 3 key principles | 1. Representative samples are better than unrepresentative samples. 2. Bigger samples are better than small ones. 3. More estimates are better than fewer estimates. |  |
| Meta-analysis | Combining the estimates of many studies instead of focusing on just one. Useful because more estimates are better than fewer. |  |
| The café example | Alex asks two friends about Café Mocha (they love it) and two about Café Latte (they don't). Her conclusion isn't reliable because of an unrepresentative sample (her friends may have unique tastes) and a small sample size (two people each isn't enough). |  |
| Statistical significance | An observed difference is statistically significant when the probability that it happened by chance is low enough to reject the null hypothesis. That means strong evidence the difference is due to something other than chance. |  |
| P-value | A number from 0 to 1 describing how likely it is that your data happened by random chance. The smaller the p-value, the less likely the results were due to chance. A p-value of .05 or less is statistically significant. | Small p = probably real. |
| Effect size | The strength of the relationship between two variables. A p-value only tells you whether an effect exists; effect size tells you how strong it is. Your slide's example: a new study technique group averaged 85 vs. 75 for the usual group, a 10-point effect. |  |
